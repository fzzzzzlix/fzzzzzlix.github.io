Little Me

Designing a wellbeing exhibition where participation had to be meaningful, not merely engaging.

The central challenge

How do you invite people to revisit childhood without knowing what childhood means to each person?

I initiated Little Me as an interactive exhibition about childhood memory, self-compassion and student wellbeing. I led the project from R&D through funding, partnerships, HR, logistics, marcom, live coordination and final reporting.

The subject made emotional engagement a risk as well as an objective. A playful prompt for one visitor could surface distress for another.

If we invited people to look inward, we also had a responsibility to think about what happened after they left the room.
Responsible design

Psychological sensitivity became an operating requirement.

R&D included content review, consultation with RMIT Wellbeing, moderation of participant-generated material and escalation planning if someone became unexpectedly overwhelmed. The aim was not to pretend triggering responses were impossible. It was to reduce foreseeable harm and make sure support existed when prevention was not enough.

Content risk

Review prompts and artworks for potentially triggering material.

Participant distress

Prepare designated support and escalation routes to RMIT Wellbeing.

Generated content

Moderate participant submissions before public display.

Beyond self-help

Make accessible professional support visible when an exhibition activity is not enough.

Experience architecture

Play → Remember → Reflect → Reframe → Make → Continue

The exhibition was designed as an emotional progression rather than a room full of unrelated activities. Entry was intentionally light: personalised name tags, a childhood buddy and optional vintage digital cameras that participants could borrow while wandering through the exhibition.

Camera demand exceeded expectations. At times there were not enough cameras for everyone who wanted one, and visitor-shot footage later became POV-style recap content.

Beyond three days

The experience should not end when the visitor leaves the room.

The exhibition included a take-home habit-building tool intended to help participants continue self-compassion and reflective practices after the live event. It also raised awareness of accessible support for people who needed more than self-guided reflection.

Social betterment requires the same operational discipline as any serious project, and often more.
Delivery and learning

Measurable reach, plus a measurement gap I would fix next time.

The three-day exhibition recorded 316 visits, including 295 student visits and 21 staff visits. Organic reach totalled 34,588 across Facebook, Instagram and TikTok. The report also recorded 22 return instances, equivalent to 6.96% of recorded visits.

The event met its attendance and communication targets, but we did not deploy a structured visitor feedback instrument. In a future iteration I would add unique attendee tracking, exit feedback, activity-level responses and source-of-awareness data.

What this proves

Social impact demanded more than good intentions.
